# Facts and sources — Tulip Mania

| Claim | Source |
|---|---|
| In 1636–37 single tulip bulbs sold in Holland for very high prices, comparable to a house | Wikipedia, "Tulip mania" — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tulip_mania ; Smithsonian, "There Never Was a Real Tulip Fever" — https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/there-never-was-real-tulip-fever-180964915/ |
| Tulips came to the Dutch from the Ottoman Empire | Wikipedia (above) |
| The prized "broken" tulips had flame-like streaks, and growers couldn't predict which bulbs would produce them | Wikipedia, "Tulip breaking virus" — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tulip_breaking_virus |
| Trading happened in taverns, over the winter, on contracts for bulbs still in the ground | Wikipedia; History Today, "Tulipmania: An Overblown Crisis?" — https://www.historytoday.com/node/54516 |
| Semper Augustus "was said to be worth a mansion" | the ~10,000-guilder figure (a grand canal house) is widely repeated but closer to legend — hence "said to be" and "(so the story goes)" |
| In early February 1637, a sale in Haarlem found no buyers; panic spread within days; buyers refused to pay | City A.M., "On this day in 1637: The tulip bubble bursts" — https://www.cityam.com/on-this-day-in-1637-the-tulip-bubble-bursts/ ; Smithsonian |
| The "country ruined" story comes largely from Charles Mackay (1841); Anne Goldgar's archive research found no bankruptcies clearly caused by tulips; the damage was to trust | Smithsonian; History Today |
| The flames were caused by a virus spread by aphids, shown by Dorothy Cayley in the late 1920s (1928) | Wikipedia, "Tulip breaking virus"; WSU HortSense — https://hortsense.cahnrs.wsu.edu/fact-sheet/tulip-break-viruses/ |

Caveat: some historians (e.g. J. G. van Dillen) describe the crash as more serious than Goldgar does; the film says "few people actually went broke", not "nobody".
Not used: the legend of the sailor who ate a bulb thinking it was an onion (Mackay).
